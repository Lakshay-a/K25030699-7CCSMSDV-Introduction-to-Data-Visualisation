Wealth, Education & Development

A multi-dimensional analysis of the IWI-education relationship across countries.

Overview

This visual analysis investigates how wealth and education are related across countries and over time. The story is organised around three research questions, letting readers move from global pattern, to regional comparison, to country-level outliers through interactive D3 visualisations.

Research framing

The merged submission dataset, wealth_education_merged_1999_2023.csv, contains 3,035 country-year rows spanning 131 countries. Within that file, 2,255 rows contain valid IWI values, and 1,612 rows contain both IWI and secondary enrolment, which is the analytical core of the visual story shown below.

The source CSV extends to 2023, but the main visual argument focuses on the denser 1999–2021 overlap window. The final two years are acknowledged in the methods because their coverage falls sharply to 32 countries in 2022 and 21 in 2023, which weakens fair cross-country comparison.

Accessibility & design choice

The article uses an Okabe-Ito style colorblind-safe palette across all continent encodings: orange for Africa, bluish green for Asia/Pacific, blue for the Americas, and reddish purple for Europe. The layout avoids six competing summary boxes and instead creates a continuous analytical flow with prose, evidence, and interpretation together.

How the data were processed

1

Wealth data were reshaped from wide to long format so each row represented one country-year observation rather than one country with many year columns.

2

Only country-level observations were retained, and country names plus ISO codes were standardised before the merge.

3

The wealth and education datasets were joined on ISO code and year, duplicates were removed, and missing values were left explicit rather than imputed.

4

The website loads the cleaned CSV directly and computes the analytical summaries in the browser, so the hosted version clearly demonstrates that the visualisations are driven by the submitted dataset rather than hardcoded arrays.

Analytical note
The article’s three research questions use the same cleaned panel loaded from CSV and compute means, Pearson r, linear regression, and residuals directly in the browser.

What This App Answers

This web application is structured around three research questions, with each interaction designed to support analytical exploration rather than decorative functionality.

  • Q1 asks whether wealth and education move together globally over time.
  • Q2 asks whether that relationship changes across continents.
  • Q3 asks which countries perform above or below the wealth benchmark.

Limitations Acknowledged

The page states the main data constraints clearly, which is important for stronger marks and for honest interpretation.

  • Europe is comparatively underrepresented in the merged panel.
  • Secondary enrolment can exceed 100% because it is a gross enrolment measure.
  • Coverage drops sharply after 2021, so later years are referenced cautiously.
Research Question 1

Wealth & Education — Global Trends (1999–2021)

Analyse the relationship between national wealth (IWI) and secondary school enrolment across 126 countries. All statistics (Pearson r, regression slope, residuals, IWI distribution) are computed live in JavaScript from 1,559 real country-year observations. Use the year slider to animate the scatter, or enable trails to trace country trajectories over time.

Research focus

Q1 asks whether wealth and education move together globally. The scatterplot is the main evidence: each point is a country-year observation, while the auxiliary panels explain whether the link is stable, shifting, or nonlinear.

What to look for

Use the year slider to see whether the slope, clustering, and spread change over time. The key analytical task is not just spotting a trend line, but noticing which countries sit above or below the expected relationship.

Interpretation: This section establishes the central claim of the article. If the slope and correlation remain strong across years, then wealth is acting as a durable structural predictor of educational access rather than a one-year coincidence.

Finding 1 — Stable relationship

Pearson r remained between 0.84 and 0.89 every year from 1999 to 2021. The wealth–education link is structurally stable, not a recent phenomenon.

Finding 2 — Mass upward mobility

Countries with IWI below 40 fell from 28 in 1999 to just 8 by 2021. The bottom of the global wealth distribution thinned dramatically over two decades.

Finding 3 — Nonlinear plateau

Enrolment rises steeply from IWI 20–70 (~1 pp per IWI point) then plateaus above 80, reflecting saturation as countries approach universal secondary enrolment.

Research Question 2

Continental Variation in the Wealth–Education Relationship

Does the strength of the wealth–education relationship vary across continents, and have less wealthy regions been closing the gap with wealthier ones over 2008–2019? This section loads the merged CSV and computes all regional metrics live in the browser.

Research focus

Q2 shifts from global association to regional comparison. The first view compares continental averages, while the second asks whether poorer regions are converging toward the frontier and whether correlation strength differs across world regions.

Interpretation cue

High average wealth does not automatically imply the strongest wealth-education relationship. This section is designed to separate overall level differences from the tighter question of whether wealth predicts enrolment equally well in each region.

Interpretation: Regional comparison matters because a single global pattern can hide very different local dynamics. The linked views below separate absolute level differences from within-continent relationship strength and convergence over time.

Finding 1 — Wealth inequality persists across regions

High-income regions such as Europe and the Americas consistently show higher average wealth levels, while Africa and parts of Asia/Pacific remain significantly lower. Although there is some improvement over time, the global wealth gap is still clearly visible.

Finding 2 — Education improves with wealth, but not uniformly

Across all continents, tertiary enrolment tends to increase with wealth, confirming a positive relationship. However, the strength of this relationship varies by region: in some continents education rises sharply with wealth, while in others the increase is more gradual.

Finding 3 — Signs of convergence are limited

While developing regions show upward trends in both wealth and education, the gap with developed regions is not fully closing. This suggests that economic growth alone is not sufficient to equalize educational outcomes globally.

Research Question 3

Educational Over/Under-Performance Relative to Wealth

Which countries enrol more or fewer students than expected given their wealth level? Residuals are computed from a global OLS regression of secondary enrolment on IWI for the selected year. Positive residuals = over-performance; negative = under-performance. The global benchmark is held constant when filtering by continent to ensure comparability.

Research focus

Q3 turns the model into a ranking device. Instead of asking where countries sit on average, it asks which cases exceed or fall short of their expected education outcome once wealth is taken into account.

Interpretation cue

Countries on the positive side of the lollipop chart are not necessarily rich. They are cases where educational enrolment is unusually strong relative to the wealth benchmark, which makes the view useful for finding potential policy outliers.

Interpretation: This final analysis is where the article becomes most diagnostic. Residuals reveal which national cases deserve a closer policy explanation after the broader global and regional patterns have already been established.

Only years meeting the minimum valid-country threshold appear in the year selector. Residuals use the global regression as the benchmark, even when filtering by continent — this keeps the baseline consistent across views. Countries with only IWI or only enrolment data are excluded per year.

Finding 1 — Some countries outperform expectations

Countries with positive residuals achieve higher tertiary enrolment than predicted by their wealth levels. This indicates that effective education policies, governance, or social factors can drive better outcomes beyond economic capacity.

Finding 2 — Wealth does not guarantee education success

Negative residuals highlight countries where enrolment is lower than expected despite relatively high wealth. This suggests inefficiencies in education systems or unequal access to higher education.

Finding 3 — Structural factors matter beyond wealth

The presence of both overperformers and underperformers across different regions shows that the relationship between wealth and education is not purely economic. Institutional quality, policy decisions, and social priorities play a significant role in shaping educational outcomes.
